How to Run a Weekly CI Review That Actually Works

A 20-minute agenda, three lists to bring and a few rules that keep the review focused on owners and next steps instead of becoming a complaint session.

Hold a short weekly review with a fixed agenda: red workflows, flaky workflows and slow workflows, each with an owner and a next step. Twenty minutes, three lists, and every item leaves with a name and a date.

Bring three lists

  1. Red: workflows whose newest default-branch run failed, with days red.
  2. Flaky: workflows that flipped three or more times in their last 100 runs.
  3. Slow: workflows with the largest p90 times multiplied by run count.

Generate them automatically before the meeting; see listing red workflows. Nobody should spend meeting time collecting data.

A 20-minute agenda

MinutesTopic
0-3Pass rate trend: better, worse, same
3-10Red list: owner and next step for each
10-15Top three flaky workflows
15-18Top slow workflow
18-20Unowned repos and decisions

Rules that keep it useful

  • Every item gets one owner and one date, or it is dropped.
  • No debugging in the meeting. Assign it and move on.
  • Skip anything that was fine last week and is fine now.
  • Cap the red list discussion: items red for more than two weeks escalate to the team lead.

Track what you decided

Keep a simple running list in an issue or a doc: item, owner, due date, status. Open it first next week. The trend line on pass rate is your scoreboard. If it does not improve after a month, the problem is not the meeting but the follow-through, or that the work is not getting time.

Share the page

A review works best when everyone can open the same page. You can point the team at a public dashboard such as Cilytics for the org view, or publish your own report as a link; see building a CI dashboard with GitHub Actions. Remember it is an hourly snapshot, so refresh before the meeting.

What good looks like after a quarter

After three months, the review should be shorter and quieter. The red list should usually be empty or contain only items under a week old. The flaky list should shrink as causes are fixed. If the meeting is still long, look at the reasons: unowned repos, workflows nobody wants to fix, or tooling that makes it hard to see the cause.

Measure the meeting's effect, not its attendance. Watch three numbers: the days the oldest red workflow has been red, the number of flaky workflows, and the pass-rate trend. If these improve, the process works. If not, change something: the owners, the time you give the work, or what you ask for.

Retire the meeting when it has nothing to do. Replace it with a posted weekly summary and an alert for a new red workflow. Meetings that exist only because they always have existed are their own kind of debt. Related reading: CI metrics that matter.

Questions people ask

Who should attend?

A platform or developer-experience lead plus one person from each team that owns a red or flaky workflow. Keep it small.

What if there is nothing red?

Skip to the trend and slow list, or cancel. A short meeting on a healthy week is a good sign.
